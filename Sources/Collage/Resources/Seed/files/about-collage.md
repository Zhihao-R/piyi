# About Collage

In 1912, Picasso glued a piece of oilcloth printed with a chair-caning pattern into a painting. *Still Life with Chair Caning* is usually taken as the start of modern collage. Before it, everything on a canvas was “painted”; after it, a canvas could hold “things that were already there.”

## What collage changed

The power of collage lies not in any single fragment but in the **seams** between them. Put a newspaper next to a scrap of wallpaper and each loses its original context; a new meaning grows out of the joint. The Dadaists used it to mock, the Constructivists to persuade, Pop Art to retell consumer culture. The technique barely changed. What changed was what the person cutting wanted to say.

## Collecting is collage too

The images you scroll past, the sentences you read, the links you save all start out scattered across different contexts. Put them on the same table and you are making a collage:

- an ukiyo-e wave next to a line from Heraclitus;
- a Bauhaus poster next to a note about empty space;
- a clip from a 1958 industrial design film next to a GIF.

The connections between them are rarely planned. You see them only after the pieces are side by side. So a good collecting tool shouldn’t only “save” and “find”. It should also make room for these **chance juxtapositions**.

## Three rules for myself

1. Don’t organize while collecting. Organizing kills connections before they form.
2. Search by description, not by folder name.
3. Once a week, browse at random, so old things get a chance to meet new ones.
